Transferability of Two-Dimensional Design Principles

Abstract
Physics-informed neural networks applied to the level-set formulation of interface ad-
vection commonly augment the residual and initial-condition losses with an eikonal
regulariser, penalising the deviation of ∥∇ϕ∥from unity. A previous two-dimensional
study identified this weight as the single most influential hyperparameter and ob-
served that its optimum shifts by four orders of magnitude between rigid-body and
strongly deforming flows, but left two questions open: whether the design principles
established there transfer to three dimensions, where collocation requirements grow
substantially, and whether the reported differences survive the run-to-run variability
that single-seed experiments cannot assess. This paper answers both. We repeat the
weight selection in three dimensions across four benchmarks (a translating sphere,
a rotating sphere, a rotating slotted sphere, and a sphere deformed and restored by
a reversed vortex), sweeping six weights with three random seeds at the full train-
ing budget under a selection rule fixed in advance. The two-dimensional ordering
is confirmed: the selected weight tracks how far the exact solution departs from
a signed distance function, spanning four decades from 10−1 where the property
holds exactly to 10−5 where the interface is stretched. The specific values, how-
ever, transfer only benchmark by benchmark; two of the four carry over from two
dimensions unchanged and two do not, so inheritance must be verified rather than
assumed. The multi-seed protocol reveals a further effect invisible to single-seed
experiments: at small weights the seed-to-seed standard deviation of the error is of
the same order as the error itself, and the regulariser reduces it by more than an
order of magnitude, so it buys reproducibility as well as accuracy. We benchmark

Introduction
The level-set method represents a moving interface implicitly, as the zero contour of a
higher-dimensional function ϕ transported by the flow [1]. Because the interface is never
meshed, topological changes such as merging and breakup require no special treatment,
which is why the formulation is used throughout multiphase flow, combustion, solidifica-
tion and image segmentation. In the simplest and most fundamental case the interface is
carried by a prescribed velocity field and ϕ satisfies a linear transport equation.
That equation is deceptively difficult to solve accurately. The quantity of interest is
not ϕ itself but its zero level set, and a scheme may reduce the error in ϕ while still
losing volume, rounding corners, or destroying thin features. It is standard practice to
initialise ϕ as a signed distance function, for which ∥∇ϕ∥= 1, because the conditioning
of the field near the interface then does not degrade; but this property is not preserved
by transport except under rigid motion, and classical solvers restore it by periodically
re-solving a separate reinitialisation equation [2, 3].
Physics-informed neural networks [4] (PINNs) offer an alternative in which ϕ is rep-
resented by a network trained to satisfy the governing equation and its initial data in a
least-squares sense at sampled collocation points. The representation is mesh-free and
continuous in space and time, so there is no Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) restriction
and no interpolation is required to evaluate the solution between grid points or time
levels. Against this, training is a non-convex optimisation whose outcome depends on
architecture, sampling and, in particular, on the relative weighting of the terms in the
composite loss. Krishnapriyan et al. [5] showed that physics-informed networks can fail on
convection-dominated problems even when the network is expressive enough to represent
the solution, so the difficulty lies in the optimisation rather than in the approximation.
Level-set advection is convection dominated by construction, which is why the configura-
tion of the training problem, and the loss weighting in particular, matters as much as it
does here.
2


1.1
Physics-informed neural networks for level-set advection
A number of studies have applied physics-informed networks to interface transport. Mullins
et al. [6] examined several architectures for moving interface problems in the level-set for-
mulation and observed that adding an eikonal regularisation term to the loss improves
the results when given an appropriate weight; they did not characterise what that weight
should be. Bi et al. [7] developed an extended-interface formulation for moving interface
problems. More recently, Zhai et al. [8] proposed a meshfree piecewise-network formula-
tion for two-phase flows with prescribed or solution-driven interface motion, with rigorous
approximation and error estimates, though without an explicit level-set representation,
and Chang et al. [9] coupled a dedicated interface-tracking network with a modified zero-
level-set function to resolve the Stefan problem, including Mullins–Sekerka instabilities.
In previous work we conducted a systematic ablation of network and loss configurations
for two-dimensional level-set advection across four benchmarks [10], establishing an ar-
chitecture and training protocol and identifying the eikonal weight as the dominant hy-
perparameter, but leaving open whether the weights established there transfer to three
dimensions.
Two threads of the wider physics-informed literature bear directly on the present work.
The first concerns where the residual is enforced: residual-based adaptive distribution
and refinement concentrate collocation points where the equation is least well satisfied
[11, 12], which matters when the solution has localised features occupying a small fraction
of the domain. The second concerns how the loss terms are balanced. Manually chosen
weights are a known source of failure [13, 5], and several schemes adapt them during
training, whether by gradient statistics [14], by trainable pointwise multipliers [15], or by
uncertainty [16]. Adaptive balancing, however, presupposes that the terms are mutually
compatible, so that driving all of them down is the correct objective. The eikonal term
does not always satisfy that premise, and identifying when it does not is the subject of
this paper.
1.2
Contributions
That extension is not automatic. Extending a two-dimensional study to three dimensions
is not merely a matter of adding a coordinate: the collocation domain grows from three
dimensions to four, so at fixed sample count the density per dimension falls sharply; the
interface becomes a surface rather than a curve, so the fraction of the domain resolving
a thin feature shrinks; and evaluation costs grow by roughly two orders of magnitude,
which constrains how much hyperparameter exploration is affordable. Whether settings
established in two dimensions remain appropriate is therefore an open question rather
than an assumption, and it is one practitioners face directly, since the natural way to
configure a three-dimensional solver is to inherit from a two-dimensional one.
3


Our previous two-dimensional study closed with two explicit open questions: whether
the design principles it established transfer to three-dimensional interface transport, and
whether its single-seed results survive an assessment of run-to-run variance [10]. This
paper answers both, and in doing so establishes the following.
1. The two-dimensional ordering transfers to three dimensions. The selected
eikonal weight tracks how far the exact solution departs from a signed distance
function, spanning four decades: 10−1 where the property holds exactly for all
time, 10−3 where it is violated locally near reentrant edges, and 10−5 where the
interface is stretched and it fails globally. This confirms in three dimensions the
shift reported in two dimensions, in a setting where the collocation density per
dimension is substantially lower.
2. The specific weights transfer only benchmark by benchmark. Two of the
four values carry over from two dimensions unchanged; the translating sphere and
the reversed vortex do not. For the translating sphere the optimum moves from
1 to 10−1, at which the two-dimensional value is three times worse. Inheritance
is therefore benchmark-dependent and must be verified rather than assumed, a
distinction the mechanism alone does not predict, since the ordering is preserved
while the values are not.
3. Multi-seed statistics change what can be concluded. With three seeds per
configuration we find that at small weights the seed-to-seed standard deviation of
the error is of the same order as the error itself, and that the regulariser reduces it
by more than an order of magnitude. Differences smaller than this spread cannot be
resolved by single-seed experiments, and one benchmark yields a nominal tie between
weights that is an artefact of that variance rather than a genuine equivalence.
4. The weight remains consequential in three dimensions.
Varying it over
four decades changes the relative L2 error by up to a factor of 31 and the volume
conservation error by up to a factor of 40, with a non-monotone response and a
well-defined interior optimum on every benchmark.
5. A comparison against a high-order finite-difference solver.
Fifth-order
WENO reconstruction with third-order total-variation-diminishing (TVD) Runge–
Kutta time stepping [17, 18], on identical grids, snapshots and error definitions.
This complements the discontinuous-Galerkin comparison reported in two dimen-
sions with a different class of classical scheme at three-dimensional resolution. The
outcome favours the classical solver on accuracy throughout, by a margin that nar-
rows with geometric difficulty.
4


6. Global norms cannot certify thin features. On the slotted sphere, replacing
the exact solution by a sphere with no slot at all changes the relative L2 error by
about one percentage point, so that measure cannot distinguish a captured slot from
an absent one. We report a sign-based, feature-restricted measure that can.
Section 2 states the formulation, the network and the training procedure. Section 3
defines the four benchmarks and the error measures. Section 4 reports the weight sweeps
and the selected configurations. Section 5 gives the comparison against the classical solver,
Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

2.3
Collocation and adaptive sampling
Collocation points are drawn uniformly in the four-dimensional domain Ω× [0, 1] and
initial-condition points uniformly in Ωat τ = 0. For the slotted sphere and the reversed
vortex the collocation set is additionally adapted during training by two mechanisms.
Residual-based adaptive distribution [11] (RAD) resamples the base set every 103 it-
erations from a pool of 2 × 104 candidates, with selection probability proportional to
|r|k/|r|k +c using k = c = 1. Residual-based adaptive refinement [12] (RAR) additionally
appends the 500 highest-residual points from a pool of 5 × 104 every 1800 iterations, so
the collocation set grows over training. The initial-condition points are not adapted.

2.5
Optimisation and training procedure
Training proceeds in two stages. Adam is run for 2 × 104 iterations at an initial learning
rate of 10−3 under a cosine annealing schedule completing one full cycle to ηmin = 10−5,
with gradient norms clipped at unity.
Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–
Shanno (L-BFGS) with a strong-Wolfe line search then refines the result. The second
stage is retained as an optional refinement rather than a required one: where the an-
nealed Adam iterate is already tightly converged the line search finds no admissible step
and the stage is inactive, whereas where convergence is incomplete it reduces the loss
substantially. Both outcomes occur across the benchmarks reported here, and the im-
provement attributable to L-BFGS is recorded for every run.

Benchmarks and error measures
Four benchmarks are used, on the unit cube Ω= [0, 1]3, ordered by the extent to which
their exact solutions depart from a signed distance function.
Translating sphere (TR3D).
A sphere of radius 0.15 centred at (0.5, 0.2, 0.5) is car-
ried by the uniform velocity u = (0, 0.1, 0) to (0.5, 0.7, 0.5) at T = 5.
Rotating sphere (RO3D).
A sphere of radius 0.15 centred at (0.5, 0.75, 0.5) is carried
by solid-body rotation about the vertical axis through (0.5, 0.5), u = (−(y −0.5), x −
0.5, 0). One full revolution completes at T = 2π, so the shape returns to its initial position
and the error at t = T measures accumulated drift over a closed orbit.
Zalesak slotted sphere (ZD3D).
The same rotation applied to a sphere of radius 0.15
from which a rectangular slot of half-extents (0.025, 0.125, 0.10) centred at (0.5, 0.725, 0.5)
has been removed. The slot has finite depth in z and is therefore a blind pocket: a section
near the pole shows an unbroken sphere while a section through the middle shows the
notch, so three mutually orthogonal sections are required to characterise the solid.

3.1
Reference solutions
For the three rigid benchmarks the exact solution is available in closed form, being the
initial field composed with the inverse of the rigid motion. The reversed vortex admits
none, so the reference is constructed by integrating the characteristics dX/dτ = Tu
backward from each snapshot with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme and evaluating
ϕ0 at the foot of each characteristic; since ϕ is advected as a passive scalar this is exact
up to the integration error. That error is bounded by a self-consistency check at t = T,
where the flow has reversed exactly and the reference must reproduce ϕ0: with 200 steps
the discrepancy is 6.3 × 10−6 in the maximum norm and 1.2 × 10−7 in the root mean
square, three to four orders of magnitude below the errors being measured.

4.1
Experimental protocol
For each benchmark, weik was swept over six logarithmically spaced values spanning four
decades, including weik = 0 as a control that removes the regulariser entirely. Each value
was trained with three random seeds (42, 43, 44), giving 18 independent training runs
per benchmark. The seed controls both network initialisation and collocation sampling,
so the reported spread covers both sources of run-to-run variability.
Every run in the sweep used the full training budget of the reported configuration: 2×
104 Adam iterations under a complete cosine annealing cycle from 10−3 to 10−5, followed
by L-BFGS with a strong-Wolfe line search. Using the production budget rather than a
shortened probe means the ranking transfers to the reported model without extrapolation
from truncated training. All other settings were held fixed at the values given in Section 2.
Component losses were logged unweighted, so runs at different weik remain comparable
term by term.
The selection rule was fixed before the sweep was run:
1. lowest seed-averaged relative L2 error;
2. tiebreak: lowest seed-averaged volume conservation error;
3. tiebreak: lowest eikonal deviation;
4. tiebreak: the smaller weight.
Two weights are treated as tied when the gap between their means is smaller than the
larger of their two seed standard deviations. Eikonal deviation is deliberately demoted to
a second-order tiebreak: it is, up to the band restriction, the very quantity that the swept
term minimises, so ranking by it would recover the sweep by construction and select the
largest weight irrespective of solution accuracy.
All metrics were evaluated on a uniform 2013 grid (∆x = 0.005) at six snapshots
t = 0, T/5, . . . , T. The eikonal deviation is restricted to a narrow band |ϕ| < 3∆x about
the interface, which excludes the gradient singularity at the centre of the sphere, where
the exact signed-distance function is not differentiable.

0.62% and 0.58%. The tie is therefore driven by instability at weik = 10−2, and weik = 10−1
dominates on both consistency and volume conservation. The criterion is reported as pre-
registered rather than revised after inspection of the results.

Rotation is a more demanding test than translation for this formulation. At every

Second-stage optimiser engagement.
The L-BFGS stage behaved differently on this
benchmark and the effect is large enough to require explicit reporting. Across the 18
runs the improvement attributable to L-BFGS, measured as the reduction in total loss
between the end of Adam and the end of L-BFGS, was bimodal rather than continuously
distributed: in 13 runs it was of order 10−8 and the wall-clock time was 12.1–12.2 minutes,
whereas in 5 runs it was of order 10−4 and the wall-clock time was 16.4–17.5 minutes. In
the latter group the strong-Wolfe line search located an admissible step from the annealed
Adam iterate; in the former it did not, and the second stage was effectively inactive. The
seed-averaged means and standard deviations in Table 3 are therefore contaminated by
this variability. For comparison, the same quantity averaged 5.70 × 10−7 across all 18
TR3D runs, where the second stage was uniformly dormant.
Stratifying by engagement leaves the selection intact. Among the five runs in which
L-BFGS engaged, the two at weik = 10−1 gave relative L2 errors of 0.143% and 0.160%,
against 0.197%, 0.419% and 0.426% at other weights. Among the thirteen runs in which
it did not, the single run at weik = 10−1 gave 0.587%, the lowest value in that group. The
selected weight is thus the best choice within each stratum separately, and the conclusion
does not depend on which optimiser regime a given run happened to fall into. Per-run
engagement figures are reported in the supplementary results file.

4.4
Zalesak slotted sphere (ZD3D)
The slotted sphere differs from the two preceding benchmarks in a way that bears directly
on the eikonal term. Its initial field is constructed as the constructive-solid-geometry
difference ϕ0 = max(ϕsphere, −ϕslot), which returns the distance to the nearer face rather
than to the edge in a neighbourhood of the reentrant edges of the slot. The exact solution
therefore does not satisfy ∥∇ϕ∥= 1 everywhere, and the eikonal term is inconsistent with
the initial condition in those neighbourhoods. The sweep grid was shifted down by one
decade accordingly.

Three features of Table 4 distinguish this benchmark from the two smooth-sphere
cases.
The regulariser remains necessary, and more so.
At weik = 0 the method fails
outright: the seed-averaged relative L2 error is 14.2% with a standard deviation of 16.4
percentage points, and one of the three seeds produced a relative L2 error of 37.5% with
a volume error of 99.998%, meaning the zero level set was lost entirely. Removing the
signed-distance constraint from a non-convex interface with thin features is not a viable
configuration.
A large weight is actively harmful.
Unlike TR3D and RO3D, where volume con-
servation was best at the largest weights tested, here it degrades monotonically once weik
exceeds 10−4: from 4.55% at 10−4 to 18.71% at 10−1, a factor of four. This is the be-
haviour anticipated by the geometry of the initial condition. Enforcing ∥∇ϕ∥= 1 near the
slot edges pulls the solution away from the exact field, and the stronger the enforcement
the further it is pulled. The same weight weik = 10−1 that is optimal on both smooth
spheres is among the worst settings here.
The metrics disagree.
This is the first benchmark on which the primary criterion and
the first tiebreak point to different weights: relative L2 is minimised at weik = 10−3 while
18


volume conservation is minimised at 10−4. The pre-registered rule resolves this in favour
of 10−3, and the per-snapshot data in Table 6 support that choice: at 10−3 the volume
error decreases monotonically over the integration window, from 8.64% to 5.78%, whereas
at 10−4 it increases from 2.24% to 6.17%. The two settings therefore end the window at
comparable volume error, while 10−3 is 40% more accurate in the field norm. The rule
also declares ties with 0 and 10−2; as in Section 4.2, the tie with 0 is an artefact of that
setting’s very large seed variance rather than a genuine equivalence.
Resolution of the slot.
At the sweep budget of Nf = 104, Ni = 5 × 103 the selected
configuration does not resolve the slot. The volume error is 5.54% and, critically, is already
6.28% at t = 0, where the initial-condition term is directly supervised and no transport
has yet occurred; a section through the slot shows the pocket rounded and roughly one
third filled. The failure is not visible in the field norm, which reads 0.606%, because the
relative L2 error is normalised by ∥ϕ∥over the whole domain and is therefore dominated
by far-field values that the network represents well. This is what motivates the feature-
restricted measures of Equation (9): a model that omits the slot entirely scores Ffill = 1,
whereas one that captures it scores Ffill ≈0, so the two cases are separated by the full
range of the measure rather than by one percentage point.
The origin is sampling rather than capacity. The slot occupies 2.36 × 10−3 of the
unit cube, so of 5×103 uniformly drawn initial-condition points approximately twelve fall
inside it. Doubling both point sets to Nf = 2 × 104, Ni = 104, with every other setting
unchanged, reduces the volume error at t = 0 by an order of magnitude, from 7.40 × 10−4
to 7.29×10−5, and the snapshot-averaged volume error from 5.54% to 0.328%; the relative
L2 field error also improves, from 0.606% to 0.480%. Increasing the Fourier feature scale
was tried and made matters worse, consistent with the constraint being the density of
supervision near a thin feature rather than the bandwidth available to represent it.
The weight sweep of Table 4 was run at the smaller budget and is reported as such;
the reported configuration of Section 4.7 uses the larger one. We re-ran the full six-weight
sweep at Nf = 2 × 104, Ni = 104 to confirm the selection transfers: the same weight
(10−3) is selected, and the statistical ties with 0 and 10−2 present at the smaller budget
disappear.

Reference solution.
The deformation field admits no closed-form solution, so the ref-
erence is constructed by integrating the characteristics dX/dτ = T u backward from each
snapshot with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme and evaluating ϕ0 at the foot of each
characteristic. Because ϕ is advected as a passive scalar this is exact up to the integration
error, which is bounded by a self-consistency check at t = T: the flow has reversed ex-
actly by that time, so the reference must reproduce ϕ0. With 200 steps the discrepancy is
6.26 × 10−6 in the maximum norm and 1.15 × 10−7 in the root mean square, three to four
orders of magnitude below the errors being measured. The reference does not depend on
the network and is computed once for the entire sweep.

roughly four times larger than TR3D while still conserving volume to better than 0.6%.
Their eikonal deviations are larger by factors of eight and forty-five respectively, which
reflects the geometry of the exact solution rather than a deficiency of the approximation.
4.8
Summary
Across all four benchmarks the eikonal weight is a consequential hyperparameter rather
than a minor regularisation detail. Varying it over four decades changes the relative L2
error by a factor of up to 31 (TR3D) and the volume conservation error by a factor of up
to 40 (ZD3D), and the response is non-monotone with a well-defined interior optimum.
The location of that optimum varies by four decades between benchmarks, from 10−1 for
a rigidly transported smooth sphere to 10−5 under deformation, and tracks how far the
exact solution departs from a signed-distance function rather than the spatial dimension.
Setting the weight by inheritance from a lower-dimensional study is therefore not reliable,
and the cost of determining it directly (18 training runs per benchmark, between four and
thirteen hours on a single T4 GPU depending on the configuration) is modest relative to
the sensitivity involved. At the selected weight the two smooth-sphere benchmarks are
solved to a relative L2 error below 0.2%, and the two whose exact solutions are not signed-
distance functions to below 0.6%, with volume conservation better than 1.2% throughout.

Comparison with a high-order classical solver
A neural solver for linear advection has to be justified against the classical schemes it
would replace. We therefore solve the same four benchmarks with a fifth-order WENO
reconstruction of the convective derivatives [17], in the level-set form of Osher and Fedkiw
[22], advanced by third-order TVD Runge–Kutta time stepping [18].
The comparison is matched wherever a choice could favour one method. Both use
the same 2013 grid, the same six snapshots, the same reference solutions and the same
error definitions of Section 3.2. Where the exact field is known it is imposed as Dirichlet
data on a three-cell boundary shell for the WENO solver; the reversed vortex requires
none, since that velocity field vanishes on the faces of the cube. No reinitialisation is
applied, so that the comparison is like for like: the network maintains the signed-distance
property through its eikonal term rather than by periodically re-solving for it, and the
eikonal deviation is reported for both.
The two methods are not commensurable in cost without care, and we report the
components separately. The classical solver is CFL limited, so its cost is set by the number
of time steps at the given resolution; the network is trained once and then evaluated, so
its cost divides into a training phase and an inference phase, the latter being a forward
pass at arbitrary points and times with no step restriction.

On smooth
rigid advection at this resolution WENO5 reaches a relative L2 error of 10−5, two orders
of magnitude below what the network attains, and a study proposing a neural solver for

The margin narrows with geometric difficulty.
The advantage narrows monoton-
ically with geometric difficulty. In the field norm the ratio of the network error to the
classical error falls from 122 on the translating sphere, to 53 on the rotating sphere, to
10 on the slotted sphere, to 4.3 on the reversed vortex. That ordering is the same one
28


that governs the eikonal weight in Section 4: the classical scheme is furthest ahead pre-
cisely where the solution is smoothest and remains an exact signed distance function, and
closest where the interface is deformed. The trend does not reverse within the four bench-
marks considered here, and we do not claim that it would; but its direction is consistent,
and it suggests that the comparison on smooth rigid advection understates the network’s
standing on harder geometries.
The gap is smaller in volume conservation.
The gap is much smaller in the con-
servation measure than in the field norm. On three of the four benchmarks the network
conserves volume to within a factor of about two of the classical scheme (2.3, 2.0 and 2.3
for RO3D, ZD3D and RV3D), against factors of 4.3 to 122 in the relative L2 error. For a
level-set method the volume of the enclosed region is the more physically meaningful of
the two, and the network is therefore closer to the classical scheme on the measure that
matters most than the field norm alone would suggest. The exception is the translating
sphere, where the network’s volume error of 1.14% is a systematic sub-cell offset in the
interface radius rather than progressive loss, as noted in Section 4.7.
An asymmetry favours the classical solver.
The comparison is matched on grid,
snapshots, reference solutions and error definitions, and neither method applies reini-
tialisation, so the classical scheme gains no level-set-specific advantage that the network
lacks. It is nonetheless not matched in one respect, and the asymmetry favours the classi-
cal solver. Where the exact field is known it is supplied to the WENO scheme as Dirichlet
data on a three-cell boundary shell, whereas the network receives no boundary informa-
tion whatsoever: it infers the far field from the residual and the initial condition alone.
The boundary data are not a convenience but a requirement for the classical scheme here.
The level-set field grows towards the domain boundary, so at an inflow face zero-gradient
extrapolation injects incorrect values that propagate inwards; without Dirichlet data the
translating sphere sits at a relative L2 error of about 36% that does not reduce under grid
refinement, even though the interface itself is transported correctly. The reversed vortex
is the exception, its velocity field vanishing on the faces of the cube so that no information
enters the domain and none is supplied.
We record this rather than adjust for it. Withholding the boundary data would hand-
icap the classical scheme rather than equalise the comparison, since a practitioner solving
these problems would have the same information available and would use it. The point is
that the accuracy reported for the classical scheme is obtained with an input the network
is never given, and that the narrowest margin in Table 9, a factor of 4.3, occurs on the
one benchmark where that input is not supplied to either method.
29


Wall-clock cost is comparable but structurally different.
Wall-clock cost is com-
parable, but the two costs are of different kinds. The classical solver is CFL limited, so its
time is fixed by the step count at the given resolution: 255 steps for the translating sphere
but 3145 for the two rotating cases, which is why it is nearly six times faster than training
on the first benchmark and roughly a third slower on the second. Network training is a
one-off cost after which evaluation is a forward pass at arbitrary points and times; the
classical solution exists only at the grid points and time levels it was marched through,
and a finer output or a different final time requires the integration to be repeated.
The case for the neural approach.
The case for the neural approach on these prob-
lems does not rest on beating a mature scheme at linear advection on a uniform grid. It
rests on properties the comparison does not measure: the representation is mesh-free and
continuous in time, requires no reinitialisation, and extends to inverse and parameter-
identification settings in which no forward solver is available. The purpose of Table 9 is
to establish the cost of those properties honestly rather than to claim they are free.
6
Discussion and limitations
6.1
Adaptive weighting
The protocol reported here determines the eikonal weight by a sweep, and a sweep is
expensive: eighteen training runs per benchmark, between four and thirteen hours on
a single GPU. This is the principal obstacle to applying the method without manual
intervention, and it was identified as such in the two-dimensional study [10]. Adaptive
weighting schemes that balance the loss terms automatically during training offer an
obvious alternative, whether based on neural tangent kernel eigenvalues [23], on gradient
statistics [13, 14], on trainable pointwise multipliers [15], or on uncertainty [16].
Two observations from the present results bear on whether such schemes can succeed
here. The first is encouraging: the results establish what the correct answer is on four
benchmarks, so an adaptive scheme can now be evaluated against a known target rather
than only against its own convergence. Reporting that an adaptive method reaches a
given accuracy is much weaker than reporting whether it recovers the optimum that a
controlled sweep identified.
The second is a caution. Adaptive balancing rests on the premise that the loss terms
are mutually compatible, so that driving all of them down simultaneously is the correct
objective. On two of our four benchmarks that premise fails. For the slotted sphere the
exact initial field is not a signed distance function near the reentrant edges of the slot,
and for the reversed vortex the advected field ceases to be one wherever the interface is
stretched; in both cases the eikonal residual of the exact solution is nonzero, and driving
30


it towards zero moves the network away from the correct answer. A scheme that equalises
gradient contributions or residual magnitudes has no mechanism for recognising that one
of the residuals it is balancing should not vanish. Whether existing adaptive methods can
recover a weight four decades below the value that maximises measured consistency is,
on this evidence, an open question rather than a foregone conclusion, and one the present
benchmarks are now equipped to settle.
6.2
Implications for sparse and noisy interface data
All four benchmarks use analytically specified initial conditions sampled without noise and
at a density chosen for convergence rather than by availability. Engineering two-phase-flow
settings differ in both respects: interface data are typically sparse, derived from imaging or
tomography, and contaminated by measurement error. The two-dimensional study set out
the extensions required before the approach can be applied to such data [10]; the three-
dimensional results reported here sharpen the picture in one specific and unfavourable
direction.
The relevant finding is the sampling behaviour of the slotted sphere reported in Sec-
tion 4.4. The slot occupies 2.36 × 10−3 of the domain volume, so of 5 × 103 uniformly
drawn initial-condition points roughly twelve fall inside it, and at that density the pocket
is approximately one third filled at t = 0, before any transport has occurred and at a time
where the initial-condition term is directly supervised. Doubling the sampling budget
reduces the volume error at t = 0 by an order of magnitude. Data density near a thin
feature is therefore not a secondary consideration but the binding constraint on whether
that feature is represented at all, and the constraint tightens with dimension: the fraction
of a domain occupied by a feature of fixed thickness falls as the dimension rises.
This has a direct consequence for sparse-data applications.
The regime in which
initial-condition data are scarce is precisely the regime in which small geometric features
are lost, and the loss is not visible in the field norm. Establishing the point at which
eikonal regularisation can no longer compensate for missing data, and characterising the
degradation under additive noise, are the two experiments we regard as necessary before
the method is applied to sparse or noisy interface data. The feature-restricted measure
introduced in Section 3.2 is a prerequisite for both, since neither degradation would be
reliably detected by the relative L2 error alone.
6.3
Threats to validity
The mechanism, and what would falsify it.
The ordering of selected weights by
departure from the signed-distance property is a claim about mechanism, not a curve
fit, and it made a prediction before the final benchmark was run: the reversed vortex, in
which the property fails globally rather than locally, should select a weight at or near zero.
31


It selected 10−5, the smallest nonzero value on the grid. The claim would be falsified by
a benchmark whose exact solution remains an exact signed distance function but which
selects a small weight, or conversely.
The second optimisation stage is not uniformly active.
On the rotating sphere
the improvement attributable to L-BFGS was bimodal across runs: in five of eighteen
the strong-Wolfe line search located an admissible step from the annealed Adam iterate
and the loss fell by four orders of magnitude more than in the remaining thirteen. The
reported means are therefore contaminated by this variability. Stratifying by engagement
leaves the selection intact, as shown in Section 4.3, but a study seeking tighter confidence
intervals would need to control it.
Global norms do not certify thin features.
The relative L2 error is normalised by
∥ϕ∥over the whole domain and is therefore dominated by far-field values. On the slotted
sphere, replacing the exact solution by a sphere with no slot at all changes it by only about
one percentage point. Volume conservation is far more discriminating and is reported
alongside throughout, but it is a signed scalar and can cancel. Any study of interfaces
with thin features should report a measure restricted to those features. Equation (9) gives
one: a pair of sign-based classification errors over the region the feature occupies, which
cannot cancel in the way a signed volume error can.
Scope.
All four benchmarks use a prescribed velocity field, so the results speak to
interface transport and not to coupled flow. Three of the four are rigid motions. The
weights reported here are established for this architecture and training protocol; whether
they transfer to substantially different networks is untested, and the evidence assembled
here suggests the question should be asked rather than assumed.
7
Conclusions
Our two-dimensional study of physics-informed level-set advection closed with two open
questions: whether the design principles it established transfer to three dimensions, and
whether its single-seed results survive an assessment of run-to-run variance [10]. We have
answered both, by repeating the eikonal weight selection in three dimensions across four
benchmarks with six weights, three seeds and the full training budget per configuration,
under a selection rule fixed before the sweeps were run.
The ordering transfers. The selected weight tracks how far the exact solution departs
from a signed distance function, across four decades: 10−1 where the property holds
exactly for all time, 10−3 where it is violated locally near reentrant edges, and 10−5 where
the interface is stretched and it fails globally. The mechanism identified in two dimensions
32


therefore survives a setting in which the collocation domain gains a dimension and the
sampling density per dimension falls accordingly.
The values do not transfer reliably. Two of the four carry over unchanged and two do
not; the translating sphere’s optimum moves by a decade, at which the two-dimensional
value is three times worse, and the reversed vortex optimal weight shifts from 10−4 in
two dimensions to 10−5 in three. The ordering is preserved while the values are not, so
inheritance from a lower-dimensional study is a hypothesis to be checked rather than a
shortcut.
Multi-seed evaluation changes what can be concluded. At small weights the seed-to-
seed standard deviation of the error is of the same order as the error itself; the regulariser
reduces it by more than an order of magnitude, so it buys reproducibility as well as
accuracy. Differences smaller than that spread are not resolvable from single runs, and
one benchmark produces a nominal tie between weights that is an artefact of variance
rather than a genuine equivalence. This is the concrete respect in which the present results
are stronger than their single-seed predecessor.
Against a fifth-order WENO solver on identical grids and error measures, the classical
scheme is more accurate on all four benchmarks, by two orders of magnitude on smooth
rigid advection. The margin narrows monotonically with geometric difficulty, from a factor
of 122 on the translating sphere to 4.3 under deformation, and is smaller throughout in
volume conservation than in the field norm, the network staying within roughly a factor
of two on three of the four benchmarks. A neural solver is not the efficient choice for
prescribed-flow advection on a uniform grid, and we do not claim otherwise; the properties
that motivate it (a mesh-free representation continuous in time, no reinitialisation, and
extensibility to inverse settings) are not measured by this comparison, and its purpose is
to price them honestly.
One methodological finding applies beyond this work.
The relative L2 error, the
standard measure in the physics-informed literature, cannot certify the preservation of
thin features: on the slotted sphere, substituting a sphere with no slot at all changes it
by about one percentage point. Studies of interfaces with fine geometric structure should
report a measure restricted to that structure, and we give one.
The natural next steps follow from the limitations set out in Section 6: evaluating
adaptive weighting schemes against the optima established here, characterising robustness
to sparse and noisy initial data, and extending to velocity fields obtained from a coupled
flow solve rather than prescribed.
